So many things to do, yet so little time. I think I need to be a bit more ambitious.

Over the past few weeks, my daily pre- and post-work pondering has become worse. It feels as if I would collapse under the weight of the thoughts I am holding at any given point. The truth is, this has been a very interesting year. The advancement of AI has reached a point where I don't think there is gonna be any job that will not feel the heat from it. And as someone who is at the closest possible vortex of the impending doom (indulge me in my dramatic undertones for some time), I can safely say, at least my job is not safe anymore.

What was my job? Well, on paper, I am a data scientist and ML engineer. I have worked on writing, coding, designing and plumbing ML-based business applications, for improving the efficiency of a system or for generating impact in terms of new customers or revenue. Mostly, the effect has been decent, I would say. Nothing cutting-edge, just good enough to help the business justify the feature. But it helped me get into the weeds of the actual system without being formally trained in it, and that became an entry point for me. I used to be very proud of the fact that I was doing some great work. I knew how to put a "Flask" over my scikit-learn classification model and then use Docker to deploy it and then make it available as an endpoint to get the results. I was happy. The company was happy. All was well.

But since the advent of AI, one thing has become very clear. I was just being lazy. I was happy, but not satisfied. Throughout my career back then, I was mostly "busy" with the "plumbing" of the systems. For me, the output and the outcome were the same. I made a model, it does not break in production, it does not break the production. I am good. I was really not involved in the decisions on the impact numbers (I was a good foot soldier), so during the initial years of my career, my understanding of whether the impact was there or not was limited. Now I do worry about them: I make sure the impact numbers are given to me as well, and I work on the versions that actually give more relevant results. I got a command and converted that into a plan, and then, using the tools available to me, I recursively solved the problem and reached the bottom of the goal. I tested each and every test case. Churned out a working piece of software. And I was good to go. I was praised, rewarded, vouched for and even looked up to. "He is the guy who can get things done; he is a generalist not bound by any domain" were some of the praises I was very happy to be associated with.

The truth is, if I continue being just that guy, I will not only be out of a job but soon be out of the tech industry altogether.

The world has changed massively over the past two years. Most of the frontier labs have made sure that both the depth and breadth of any domain-specific problem that has well-defined constraints should be, and must be, solved by their models. And they have been very successful at it too.

AI went from not knowing how to solve a new problem because it's not part of their training data ([Apple's paper on why models can't reason](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/illusion-of-thinking)), to now being able to think through a new problem where the constraints are well defined, with the guidance of a human ([the Navier–Stokes result](https://www.quantamagazine.org/ai-has-solved-one-of-maths-1-million-millennium-prize-problems-20260908/)) and sometimes without one ([OpenAI's post on the math problems its model has solved](https://openai.com/index/ten-advances-in-mathematics/)).

This is a remarkable pace. And a very scary proposition. Think for a second. Gone are the days when knowing a certain tech stack would get you a job at FAANG or an upcoming startup. Gone are the days when you could just mug up a few already-solved and heavily reused system design problems to pass an interview. And reimplement the same CRUD-style applications and get paid good money for it. Gone are the days when you could just do `model.fit(x, y)` or `import tensorflow as tf`, write 2–3 lines of a dense or sparse neural network with a predefined and chosen loss function for your classification or regression, and call yourself a data scientist. Gone are the days when you could just link Tableau to a data source and that was your job as a data analyst or a BI engineer.

And thank god those days are gone. Thank god my job is finally free from all this plumbing. To be fair, I want to make this clear: you should still have the general idea of how it works. But I am glad we don't need to do this by hand anymore. Now I am free to actually delve deeper into the nuances and the depth of my domain. It is true for everyone. Now is the time to go all in. Or to cash out. The table is set; the gamble is already on. You have your cards and AI has its own. For now, they are winning because both our jobs were predictable. They caught our tell; they basically learned quickly how we won, and not only replicated it but actually outsmarted us. On multiple turns.

But they were only able to do that because we were risk-averse. We were too scared to go deeper. We thought that the abstraction layer of packages, or the implementation of a paper from a blog post that we just replicate, is the level we should play at. Or worse, we thought that is the level we deserved. But that is not true anymore. AI is the great leveller. It has proven that it's far more than capable of doing a staff engineer's job better than a human could, with the right guidance. It can both automate and replicate what you and I do. So now there is no option but to let go of our inhibitions. It's time to go ALL IN. It's time to take the plunge and go deeper. Thanks to AI, now it's gonna be guided. Still uncertain. Still dark, but not without a good, experienced driver. All in ALL, it's time to master this tool and make yourself truly who you are. A scientist. An engineer. Not a tech plumber. Because that job is taken.
